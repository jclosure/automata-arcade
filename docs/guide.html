Field Guide — v1.0

Tools of a God.
For the Curious.

Automata Arcade puts Conway's Game of Life in your hands as a living laboratory. Draw cells, drop patterns, rewind time, document discoveries, and watch simple rules produce complex, beautiful, surprising worlds — all in your browser, with no setup.

No install needed Runs in browser Zero prior knowledge required Beginner → Expert
The Idea

What exactly is happening here?

Every cell on the board is alive or dead. Every generation, each cell looks at its eight neighbours and follows one rule: three neighbours = be born, two or three = survive, anything else = die. That's it. That's the entire physics of this universe.

The Three-sentence explanation

You're watching a universe run on three simple rules. Those rules produce gliders, guns, computers, even digital life forms. Your job is to experiment, break things, and document what you find.


Why does any of this matter?

Conway's Game of Life is Turing-complete — meaning it can, in principle, compute anything a regular computer can. Scientists use it to study emergence, evolution, and self-organization. You can use it to just have fun watching things explode.


No experience needed

You don't need to know anything about cellular automata, computation, or mathematics. Start by clicking cells and pressing Play. Curiosity is the only prerequisite.

Quick Start

Your first five minutes

Follow these five experiments in order. By the end you'll have seen the core magic and be ready to explore on your own.

1

Press Load Demo

The Load Demo button sets up a live crossfire between two Gosper Glider Guns. Press Play (or the spacebar) and watch. Two streams of gliders are heading toward each other — you've just loaded a NOT gate.

2

Rewind to the beginning

Hit the ⏮ button at the bottom of the screen. The board snaps back to generation 0. Now drag the scrubber slowly to the right and watch the collision replay frame by frame.

3

Draw something

Make sure ✏ Paint mode is selected (top bar). Left-click and drag anywhere on the dark grid to paint new cells. Press Play and watch your cells interact with the existing patterns.

4

Drop a prefab

In the Prefab Palette on the left, drag any card — try the Glider — directly onto the board. Release to drop it. Press Play and watch it sail across the canvas.

5

Save a discovery to the Notebook

Click the 📓 Notebook button in the top bar. Click + Entry, type a title like "First run", tick Snapshot board, and click Save Entry. That moment is now preserved forever.

Every pattern in the palette can be oriented before you drop it. Use the Rotate selector in the controls row to spin it 0°, 90°, 180°, or 270° (or tap R to cycle). Use Flip X (or F) to mirror it.

Forking history

If you scrub back to, say, generation 50 and press Play, all history after generation 50 is discarded and a fresh timeline grows from that point. Think of it like choosing a different fork in time.


Slow-motion tip

Pause the simulation. Use N to step forward one frame at a time. This is the best way to study a specific collision — a glider hitting an eater, two streams annihilating, a chaotic patch suddenly crystallising into a still life.

The Notebook

No other Game of Life tool has ever had this. The Notebook is a living research journal built directly into the simulation — temporally anchored, spatially aware, and self-writing.

The Notebook panel open alongside the board, showing the Live Feed and two journal entries.

Click the 📓 Notebook button in the top bar to open the panel. It slides in from the right. Click it again — or press the ✕ inside the panel — to close it.

📡 Live Feed

The system watches your simulation automatically. It logs population peaks, extinction events, near-extinction moments, and stabilisation without any input from you. The feed updates in real time as you run.

📖 Journal Entries

Create your own entries at any moment. Each entry is locked to the current generation. Click ↺ restore on any entry to teleport the entire simulation — board, camera, generation — back to that exact moment.

📍 Spatial Pins

Drop numbered pins on specific spots on the board. Pins are anchored in world coordinates: they stay fixed as you pan and zoom. Perfect for labelling a gun, an eater, or a collision zone.

◆ Scenes & Cinematics

Mark any entry as a "scene". Press ▶ Scenes to play them back as a cinematic — the simulation restores each moment and plays forward for 8 seconds before transitioning to the next.

Creating a journal entry

You can create an entry at any point — paused, playing, at any generation. Each entry captures the moment you press Save.

  • Title — Give the moment a name. "Crossfire gen 47", "Beautiful asymmetric crash".
  • Notes — Write anything: observations, questions, hypotheses.
  • Snapshot board — When checked, the full board state is saved as an RLE snapshot. The entry can restore the exact board later, even in a new browser session.
  • Mark as scene — Includes this entry in the cinematic playback.

Press Save Entry or hit Enter in the title field. A coloured dot immediately appears on the timeline scrubber at this entry's generation.

The entry form open with a title and notes ready to save.

Timeline dots — your journey mapped

Every entry you create — including auto-detected events — places a coloured dot on the Time Machine scrubber at the exact generation it was recorded.

Hover a dot to see the entry title. Click any dot to immediately restore that entry — the board jumps back to that moment, the camera repositions, and the generation counter updates. Your entire exploration history is mapped onto the timeline.

Why this is powerful

Instead of hunting for "that moment when everything went wrong", you can drop an entry the instant something interesting happens. The dot is your permanent bookmark in time.

Two amber-tinted and teal notebook markers on the timeline scrubber. The amber dot is a user entry; the teal dot is an auto-detected event.

The Live Feed — self-writing observations

You don't have to write everything yourself. The Live Feed watches your simulation every 30 generations and logs significant events automatically:

  • Population peak — when the board reaches a new high of live cells
  • Near-extinction — when fewer than 8 cells remain
  • Extinction — when the last cell dies
  • Stabilisation — when population locks for 20+ consecutive generations

Each auto-event is also added to the journal list with a muted auto badge so you can tell them apart from your own entries.

Let it run overnight

Drop a seed pattern (try Acorn from the Circuit palette), press Play at high speed, and leave the tab open. When you return, the Live Feed will have narrated 5,000+ generations of history.

The Live Feed after running the demo for a few seconds. Auto-detected events populate automatically.

Cinematic scene playback

The most theatrical feature in the app. Mark any journal entries as scenes, then press ▶ Scenes to watch them play out like a film.

  • For each scene, the board restores to that exact state (camera included)
  • The simulation plays forward for 8 seconds
  • The scene title and notes appear in a floating overlay
  • After 8 seconds it transitions to the next scene automatically
  • Press ✕ Stop at any time to end the cinema
Mark scenes with snapshots

For the cinematic to restore the exact board, the entry needs a board snapshot. Always check Snapshot board when creating entries you plan to use as scenes.

An entry marked as a scene — the purple ◆ badge shows it's included in cinematic playback.

Guided Learning

Circuit Academy

Seven structured lessons that teach signal logic from scratch — no prior knowledge needed. Each level comes with an on-screen guide card that explains the concept and offers three progressive hints on demand.

What you'll learn

  • CA-1 Stream Rider — Gliders as signal packets. Watch a Gosper gun fire and understand what c/4 diagonal speed means.
  • CA-2 Signal Stop — The Eater-1. Place one in a live stream and watch it absorb every glider without dying.
  • CA-3 Parallel Streams — Two independent gun streams ignore each other completely — signals don't collide unless their paths actually overlap.
  • CA-4 Not Today — Head-on annihilation. Two opposing streams cancel every packet. This is a NOT gate.
  • CA-5 OR Gate — Two inputs, either one wins. Block one gun — the other still delivers.
  • CA-6 AND Gate — Both inputs must fire. Block either gun — neither receptor fires.
  • CA-7 Signal Crossing — SE and SW streams travel through the same board region on completely different diagonal rails. They never interact.

CA-1 Stream Rider — the guide card explains glider streams. The Hint button reveals progressive clues.


The guide card lives in the bottom-left corner of the canvas. Close it with × when you're ready to work independently.

Topology

3D Manifold Modes

Swap the Mode dropdown to Sphere, Torus, Klein Bottle, Möbius Strip, or Cylinder. The same Game of Life rules run on curved surfaces rendered in WebGL.

Why play on a curved surface?

On a flat grid, every glider that reaches the edge disappears. On a Torus, it wraps around and re-enters from the opposite side. On a Klein Bottle, it enters flipped. On a Möbius Strip, the topology itself becomes part of the game.

  • Sphere 3 — no edges, fully closed surface
  • Torus 4 — wrap left↔right and top↔bottom
  • Klein Bottle 5 — left/right wrap, top/bottom flip
  • Möbius Strip 6 — left/right wrap with Y-flip, open edges
  • Cylinder 7 — left/right wrap, open top/bottom
Drawing in 3D

Left-click on the surface to paint cells. Right-drag to rotate the view. Scroll to zoom. All surface modes respect the same GoL rules — topology only affects neighbour lookup at the boundaries.

Torus mode. A glider near the right edge will re-enter from the left. Drop a Gosper gun pointing right — the stream wraps around and could hit the gun itself.

Step-by-Step

Mini Tutorials

Each tutorial takes 3–8 minutes and teaches a real skill you'll use again and again.

Tutorial 1

Build a self-sustaining glider stream

You'll set up a Gosper Glider Gun, point it at an Eater, and prove that signals can be absorbed cleanly — forever.

1

Clear the board

Press C to clear, then press G to toggle the grid on so you can see individual cells.

2

Drop a Gosper Glider Gun

In the Prefab Palette (Required category), drag the Gosper Glider Gun card onto the left side of the board. Drop it there.

3

Run for 60 generations

Press Play and let it run to about generation 60. Watch gliders firing diagonally to the lower-right. Pause.

4

Drop an Eater

From the palette (Required category), drag Eater-1 directly into the stream — somewhere along the diagonal path the gliders are travelling. The Eater's "bite" corner must face the incoming gliders (rotate with R if needed).

5

Watch interception

Press Play. The Eater absorbs every glider and bounces back to its original shape. It will keep doing this indefinitely. You've built signal termination.

6

Record it

Open the Notebook, create an entry titled "Glider stream termination", check Snapshot, and save. You now have a permanent record of this working circuit.

Tutorial 2

Rewind and study a chaotic crash

Use the Time Machine to find and freeze a specific moment of chaos — the most powerful debugging tool in the simulation.

1

Load the Demo

Click Load Demo. Two guns, an eater, several seeds — a complex scene guaranteed to produce interesting collisions.

2

Play at high speed

Set the speed slider (bottom-right of timeline) to 15× or higher. Press Play and watch for a crash or chaotic bloom.

3

Pause at the interesting moment

As soon as something surprising happens, press Spacebar to pause.

4

Step backward to the exact frame

Press B repeatedly to step back one generation at a time. Find the precise moment the crash began.

5

Step forward in slow motion

Press N to advance one generation at a time. Watch each cell live or die. You can see exactly which neighbours triggered each birth or death.

6

Pin the discovery

With the board paused at the crash frame, open the Notebook, click 📍 Pin, click on the collision point on the board, write "Glider met the edge of the seed bloom" and save.

Tutorial 3

Create your first cinematic

Document a complete story — from empty board to stable pattern — and play it back as a film.

1

Clear and plant a seed

Press C to clear. From the Circuit palette, drag Acorn to the centre of the board. This tiny 7-cell pattern will run for 5,206 generations before stabilising.

2

Save entry: "Act 1 — The Seed"

Open the Notebook, click + Entry. Title: "Act 1 — The Seed". Body: "Acorn planted at centre. 7 cells. 5,206 generations to go." Check Snapshot. Check Mark as scene. Save.

Run to the chaos peak

Set speed to 15× and play until around generation 200, when the board is at maximum chaotic bloom. Pause.

4

Save entry: "Act 2 — Maximum Chaos"

Create another entry. Title: "Act 2 — Maximum Chaos". Check Snapshot and Mark as scene. Save.

Set speed to 30× and let it run until the Live Feed reports "Board stabilized". The Acorn eventually resolves into a collection of still lifes, oscillators, and gliders.

Save entry: "Act 3 — Peace"

One last entry. Title: "Act 3 — Peace". Body: whatever you observed. Check Snapshot and Mark as scene. Save.

Press ▶ Scenes

Tutorial 4

Play Life on a doughnut

Switch to Torus mode and see what changes when your universe wraps around itself with no edges.

1

Switch to Torus mode

Press 4 or use the Mode dropdown. The canvas switches to a 3D torus rendered in WebGL.

2

Paint a glider near the right edge

Left-click on the torus surface to paint cells. Draw the classic glider shape (3×3, L-shape) close to the right edge of the torus.

3

Press Play and watch it wrap

The glider reaches the right edge — and re-enters from the left. The torus has no boundary. The glider will travel forever in an eternal loop.

Drop a Gosper gun near the top edge

Drag a Gosper Gun card from the palette. Let it fire. The stream exits the top of the torus and re-enters at the bottom — it could circle around and hit the gun itself.

Recommendations

Getting the best results

These are the habits that separate casual exploration from genuine discovery.

📍

Pin first, explore later

When something interesting appears, drop a Notebook pin on it immediately — before you move the camera or advance another generation. You can always write the notes later, but you can't recover the board position.

🐢

Slow motion is your microscope

Use N to step one generation at a time when studying a collision. The difference between a glider annihilating and a glider surviving is often a single cell offset — you can't see it at full speed.

📸

Always snapshot scenes

When creating entries you want to use in the cinematic, always check "Snapshot board". Without a snapshot, the Scenes player can only restore the camera — not the board state itself.

🧪

Use Circuit Academy as a library

The 7 Circuit Academy levels are runnable at any time. Load CA-4 just to watch the NOT gate in action. Load CA-7 to see the signal crossing. You don't have to "complete" them — use them as demonstrations.

⏪

Rewind before you erase

Before pressing C to clear, consider whether there's a state in the history buffer worth saving as a Notebook entry. Once you clear, that history is gone.

🌀

Try seeds, not guns, for chaos

Gosper Guns produce predictable, periodic output. For wild emergent behaviour, drop a R-Pentomino or Acorn from the Circuit palette, stand back, and watch the universe figure itself out.

🎬

Build your scenes chronologically

The Scenes cinematic plays entries in generation order. Plan your three-act structure: an early state, a peak moment, a resolution. Create entries at each act, then hit Scenes to watch the story.

🌐

Topology changes everything

A pattern that dies on a flat grid might be immortal on a torus. A glider gun that wastes its stream into empty space on the plane might create a closed loop on a cylinder. Try any pattern in 3D mode — the surprises are real.

Advanced: Rule Lab

In the Inspector panel on the right, the Rule Lab section lets you change the physics of life itself. Try B36/S23 (HighLife) for a self-replicating pattern. Try B2/S (Seeds) to watch explosive growth with nothing surviving. Try B3678/S34678 (Day & Night) for a universe where dead and alive are symmetric. Always create a Notebook entry before changing the rule — so you can restore your favourite state later.

The deepest insight

The most important thing to understand about Conway's Game of Life is that complexity is free. The three rules that govern every cell are simpler than a traffic light. The patterns that emerge from them — spaceships, guns, computers, universal constructors — were not designed. They were discovered. This is a universe where the laws are trivially simple and the consequences are infinite. You are now one of the people exploring it.
